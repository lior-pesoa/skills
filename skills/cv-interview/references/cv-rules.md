# CV rules, with their evidence

Rules for the draft and the final CV. Each rule names its source and how strong the evidence is.

**Evidence key.** *Moderate*: consistent across several studies, or one large experiment. *Weak*: expert opinion or old surveys. *Vendor*: research by a company that sells CV services. *Law*: statute text. CV research is thin and mostly old, so treat Weak and Vendor rules as sensible defaults, not proven facts.

## Structure

Reverse-chronological, with a targeted top section (a "hybrid"):

1. Name and contact details (in the body of the page, not a Word header or footer)
2. Target headline: the role they want
3. Summary, 2–4 lines
4. Relevant highlights: 3–5 evidence bullets, each naming where it happened (optional; strongest for career changers)
5. Experience, newest first. In each role, the bullets most relevant to the target come first. Older or unrelated roles are condensed to a line or two.
6. Training and projects (above education when it's the bridge into a new field)
7. Education
8. Skills, languages and tools

- Chronological beats functional (skills-only) CVs, which recruiters dislike. *Moderate.* Risavy (2017), "The resume research literature", *Journal of Educational and Developmental Psychology* 7(1), doi:10.5539/jedp.v7n1p169; Behavioural Insights Team (2021) CV trial report.
- Recruiters spend most of their first look on titles, companies and dates, so the target headline and summary are the only place to reframe. *Vendor.* TheLadders eye-tracking study (2012).

## Length

- One or two pages, never more. Two is fine for about 10+ years of relevant experience. Page one must stand on its own. *Moderate.* Risavy (2017).
- Israeli job sites prefer one page in Hebrew, two at most in English. *Weak.* AllJobs, Drushim, Nefesh B'Nefesh guides.

## Summary

- 2–4 lines: the target role, the 2–3 strongest strengths with evidence elsewhere in the CV, and, for a career change, the bridge. *Weak.* Risavy (2017); Thoms et al. (1999).
- No "I", no clichés, no self-ratings such as "excellent communicator". Self-praise lowers perceived truthfulness and likeability; moderate self-promotion helps. *Moderate.* Knouse et al. (1988); Knouse (1994); Waung et al. (2017), *Journal of Business and Psychology* 32(6), doi:10.1007/s10869-016-9470-9.

## Bullets

- Accomplishments, not duties. Specific beats vague. *Moderate.* Thoms, McMasters, Roberts & Dombkowski (1999), *Journal of Business and Psychology* 13(3), doi:10.1023/A:1022974232557; Risavy (2017).
- Start with a verb, one idea per bullet, 1–2 lines (about 25 words at most). *Moderate.* Risavy (2017), citing several older studies.
- Pattern: what they did → for whom or at what scale → result. Include a result only when they gave one.
- 3–5 bullets for recent relevant roles, 0–2 for older or unrelated ones.
- Numbers: no experiment isolates their effect, but they're a form of specificity, so ask for them. Never estimate one. *Weak.*

## Skills and keywords

- Most employers' systems filter and rank on exact criteria, so use the job ad's exact words where they're true. *Moderate.* Fuller et al. (2021), *Hidden Workers*, Harvard Business School and Accenture.
- Applicant tracking systems rarely auto-reject for formatting; knockout questions are the real filter. *Vendor.* Enhancv (2025), interviews with 25 US recruiters.
- Skills line: concrete, checkable items (tools, methods, languages), listed the way they're actually used. Soft skills belong in bullets as evidence, not in a list.
- Employers distrust self-assessed skills and prefer evidence. *Weak.* Nesta for City of London Corporation (2019), *Transferable Skills in the Workplace*.

## Design

- Formal, plain, single column. Every departure from a formal layout lowered interview chances in one experiment. *Moderate.* Arnulf, Tegner & Larssen (2010), *European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology* 19(2).
- Black or very dark text on white, one restrained accent at most, no photo, icons, skill bars or sidebars. Body 10.5–11 pt, headings 12–14 pt, line spacing about 1.25–1.4, margins 15–20 mm. A4 for Israel and Europe, US Letter for the US. *Weak.* Butterick, *Practical Typography*; AllJobs.
- Export a text-based PDF (selectable text) and a .docx. Workday, Lever and Greenhouse accept both; image-only PDFs fail. *Weak* (vendor documentation).

## Leave out

- Photo. In an Israeli field experiment with 5,312 CVs, women without a photo got more callbacks than women with one. *Moderate.* Ruffle & Shtudiner (2015), *Management Science* 61(8), doi:10.1287/mnsc.2014.1927.
- Date of birth, age, marital status, children, ID number, full street address (city is enough), religion, health. Age and attractiveness discrimination are at least as severe as ethnic discrimination. *Strong (meta-analysis).* Lippens, Vermeiren & Baert (2023), *European Economic Review* 151. In Israel, the Equal Employment Opportunities Law (1988) bans hiring discrimination on most of these grounds, and s. 2A bans asking for the military medical profile. *Law.*
- Graduation years more than about 15 years ago, and detail on roles older than about 15 years. *Weak.* AARP guidance.
- "References available on request" and objective statements ("Seeking a challenging position"). *Weak.*

## Gaps and dates

- Show years, not months, by default. A UK trial with 9,022 real applications found that showing years of experience instead of dates raised callbacks for returners after a caring break. *Moderate (one trial).* Behavioural Insights Team (2021).
- Never invent something to fill a gap. Name it plainly (study, project, caring) when they want to.

## Career changers

- A short, sensible reason for the move helps. *Vendor.* Career Advisory Board (2016), 500 US hiring managers.
- Frame the move as a next step, showing the overlap between old and new work. Similar-but-not-identical moves are hired more often than unrelated jumps. *Moderate.* Leung (2014), *American Sociological Review* 79(1).
- Describe past work by the tasks the target role shares, not by old job titles. *Moderate.* Gathmann & Schönberg (2010), *Journal of Labor Economics* 28(1).

## Honesty

- 58% of US hiring managers had caught a lie on a CV, and half would dismiss a candidate for it. *Vendor.* CareerBuilder / Harris Poll (2014).
- CV fraud (fabrication, embellishment, omission) predicts worse job performance. *Moderate.* Henle, Dineen & Duffy (2019), *Journal of Business and Psychology* 34, doi:10.1007/s10869-017-9527-4.
- Since AI writing tools spread, polished but generic applications carry less signal, and time spent editing the AI draft was linked to hiring. *Moderate (working papers).* Galdin & Silbert (2025/26), "Making talk cheap"; Cui, Dias & Ye (2025), arXiv:2509.25054.
- Writing help that improves clarity, without adding content, raised hires by 8% in a randomised trial of nearly 500,000 jobseekers. *Moderate.* Wiles, Munyikwa & Horton (2025), *Management Science* 71(12).

So: reframing (emphasis, order, wording, the target field's vocabulary) is allowed. Changing facts is not.

## Checks before delivery

- Spelling and grammar. Two errors lowered interview probability by about 7 percentage points, five errors by about 18. *Moderate.* Sterkens et al. (2023), *PLOS ONE* 18(4), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0283280.

## Myths: don't repeat these to the person

- "Recruiters spend 6 seconds on a CV": a vendor figure for the first fit/no-fit glance, not reading time.
- "ATS reject 75% of CVs": traced to a CV-optimisation company, no method published.
- "ATS can't read PDFs": they can; image-only PDFs are the problem.
- "A CV must be one page": the evidence supports one or two.
- "Functional CVs are best for career changers": the evidence points the other way.
